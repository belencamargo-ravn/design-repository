# Design & Product at Ravn — A Reflection

2026-09-22 · @Belen Camargo

## What design owns

Design owns the how: the craft of turning a validated need into an experience people actually use — information architecture, interaction patterns, visual language, and whether a flow feels effortless or confusing. That ownership extends to the vocabulary of a screen itself — what a trust signal, a badge, or an entry point looks like and where it sits — because those are craft decisions that should be shaped by design judgment and user research, not handed down as a spec.

## What product contributes across the lifecycle

Product brings the why and the constraints design can't see alone: which problem is worth solving right now, what users and the business actually need, the trade-offs behind scope and pricing, and the metrics that tell both sides afterward whether a decision worked. That contribution runs the whole lifecycle — framing the problem at discovery, setting the boundaries at scoping, reading the data after launch — but it's a contribution of context and priorities, not of pixels. When it crosses into specifying how something should look or where it should sit, it stops being product's job and starts being someone else's, done badly.

## Reframing a decision: solution → problem

Two moments from my own practice show the slip from framing into prescribing.

**Candidate 1 — Verification badges.** What I said: the badge should look like this and sit here. Reframed as a problem: "Requesters need to trust a stranger enough to book them, quickly, without reading paragraphs of proof — how might design surface that confidence at the moment someone is choosing a provider?"

**Candidate 2 — Add-to-purchase (ReNest).** What I said: put the option here, make it look like this. Reframed as a problem: "Users need a fast, low-friction way to add an item to their purchase list from wherever they discover it — what's the least-friction path to that, and where does it actually belong in the flow?"

Both started from a real, valid need. The mistake wasn't having an opinion — it was skipping past the problem statement and handing design the solution already assembled.

## Conclusion

PMs are facilitators and colleagues of designers, not bosses. Neither role is superior to the other, and success is achieved when both work side by side toward the same objective, with clear communication carrying the work between them.
